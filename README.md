BRIDGING THE GAPS IN GHANA'S NATIONAL HEALTH INSURANCE SCHEME

A Health Policy Analysis by Dr. Emmanuel Kwame Akuamoah

Introduction

Hello, and welcome. My name is Emmanuel Kwame Akuamoah, a Medical Officer at Suntreso Government Hospital in Kumasi, Ghana. I have been working on the frontlines of clinical medicine for the past four years, seeing patients every day across a wide range of conditions (from routine illnesses to life-threatening emergencies). In that time, I have also seen something that doesn't show up in any patient's chart: the quiet, daily struggle that people go through just to access the care they need. Before I became a doctor, I grew up watching family members navigate a health system that felt, at times, like it was built for someone else. That personal history, combined with my clinical work, is what drives my passion for health equity i.e. the idea that where you were born, how much money you have, or what kind of work you do should not determine whether you live or die from a treatable condition. Today, I want to talk about a system that was designed with exactly that goal in mind and why, despite its promise, it is still falling short for too many Ghanaians.

The Problem: A Promising System With Deep Cracks

Ghana made history in 2003 when it launched the National Health Insurance Scheme, or NHIS. The idea was revolutionary for Sub-Saharan Africa at the time. To create a system where every Ghanaian, regardless of income, could access healthcare without paying out of pocket at the point of service. Before the NHIS, Ghana operated what people bitterly called the "cash and carry" system. In this system, anytime you showed up sick, you paid first, or you didn't get care. Families sold livestock, took out loans, or simply stayed home and hoped for the best. The NHIS was meant to end all of that.
And in many ways, it has made a real difference. Enrollment has grown significantly over the years, and studies have shown that NHIS members are more likely to seek care early, before conditions become serious. Maternal and child health outcomes have particularly improved in areas with high NHIS coverage. So, this is not a story about a failed policy. It is a story about a good policy with serious implementation gaps. Gaps that, if we are honest, fall hardest on the people who needed the scheme most in the first place.

Here is the core problem: the NHIS looks like universal health coverage on paper, but it does not function as universal health coverage in practice. There are three major reasons for this, and they feed into each other in ways that make the problem self-reinforcing. 

The first is financial. The NHIS is funded partly through a levy, which is a small tax added to purchases of goods and services, and partly through premiums paid by informal sector workers, who are people working outside of formal employment like market traders, farmers, and artisans. But many of these workers live in poverty, and when it's time to renew their NHIS card each year, they simply don't have the money. According to Ghana Health Service reports and independent health policy research, a significant share of the population is either unregistered or lapsed, meaning they have an expired card and are effectively uninsured.

The second problem is on the supply side (the healthcare facilities themselves). Even when patients have valid NHIS cards, many facilities are reluctant to provide certain services or medications under the scheme because the government is slow to reimburse them. Delayed reimbursements (sometimes stretching months) put hospitals and clinics in a difficult financial position. They have salaries to pay, medications to stock, and equipment to maintain. When the money doesn't come, facilities start to ration care, turning away NHIS patients or charging informal top-up fees that are supposed to be illegal. I have seen this firsthand. I have watched colleagues face the impossible choice of turning someone away or providing care their facility may not be compensated for. That is not a personal failing. It is a system failure. 

The third problem is location oriented and structural. Healthcare facilities are not evenly distributed across Ghana. Urban areas like Accra and Kumasi have more hospitals, more specialists, and more services covered by the NHIS. Rural communities (often the poorest) are furthest from care and face the worst of these access gaps.

Who Is Being Left Behind?

The most affected are informal sector workers (majority of Ghana's labor force) These are the women selling tomatoes at Kejetia Market, the motorcycle taxi riders, farmers in the Brong-Ahafo region. They have no employer to facilitate their insurance enrollment, no paycheck deductions to keep their coverage current. Research published by Ghanaian health economists shows that premium costs represent a significant barrier for households earning just enough to eat. When you are choosing between renewing your NHIS card and feeding your children this week, the card will always lose.

Closely linked to this group are pregnant women and young children in poor households. Ghana offers free NHIS coverage for pregnant women and children under five. But the benefit only works if women know about it, if they live close enough to a facility to use it, and if that facility actually has the drugs and services covered under the scheme. In practice, drug stock-outs (meaning essential medications simply running out) are a persistent problem. A woman covered by NHIS may still be told to buy her own malaria drugs or her prenatal vitamins from a pharmacy, out of pocket. It turns a free benefit into a partial one.

I would be wrong if I did not mention healthcare workers themselves as people affected by this problem. Delayed reimbursements mean facilities operate with chronic resource shortages. That translates into staff who are overworked, under-equipped, and demoralized. Burnout among Ghanaian health workers is real, and it contributes to the brain drain. Every skilled health worker who leaves is a loss that patients feel directly.

Dreaming Big: What an Equitable Solution Would Look Like

Now, here is my favorite part. If we set aside funding constraints and political inertia for a moment, what would a truly equitable solution look like? I want to be clear that I am not describing a fantasy. Every element I am about to propose has been implemented somewhere in the world. The question is not whether it's possible. The question is whether we have the political will and the financing to do it.

First and most urgently: fix the reimbursement system. This is foundational. If facilities are not paid reliably and promptly, everything else collapses. Ghana should move toward a digitized, real-time claims and reimbursement platform, similar to what Rwanda has implemented under its Mutuelle de Santé insurance system. Every service rendered to an NHIS patient gets logged digitally at the point of care. Claims are processed automatically, with reimbursement cycles capped at 30 days. This removes the cash flow crisis at the facility level, which in turn removes the incentive to turn patients away or charge illegal fees. Rwanda's experience shows this is achievable even in a resource-limited context.

Second: Use technology to make enrollment easier. We live in a country where mobile money(e-transactions) has transformed commerce in a decade. We should apply the same innovation to NHIS enrollment. A fully mobile NHIS platform would let informal workers register, renew, and check their coverage status from their phones. Community health workers should be equipped and compensated as NHIS enrollment agents, bringing the scheme directly to the people who are hardest to reach.

Third, and perhaps most important for long-term sustainability: invest in a robust essential medicines supply chain. Coverage is meaningless if the medicines aren't there. A nationally coordinated essential drug procurement system, i.e. buying in bulk, distributing equitably to facilities based on population need, and tracking stock levels in real time would go a long way toward closing the gap between what NHIS promises and what patients receive. This is a matter of equity. Currently, well-resourced urban hospitals are much more likely to have NHIS-covered drugs in stock than rural health centers. That geographic inequality is not inevitable. It is a distribution and financing choice.

Who Needs to Be at the Table?

None of this happens in a vacuum, and none of it can be done by one person or one institution. The Ministry of Health sets policy direction and budget priorities at the national level. They have to be in the room and they have to be accountable. The Ghana Health Service manages the delivery of care across the country and understands, on the ground, where the system is breaking down. The National Health Insurance Authority is the body that actually runs the scheme: enrollment, reimbursements, and coverage decisions all flow through them. If reimbursements are delayed by months, that is an NHIA operational problem that needs an NHIA solution, with political backing from the Ministry.

But I want to push back gently on something that often happens in health policy conversations: we spend a lot of time talking about governments and institutions, and not enough time talking about the people closest to the problem. Hospital administrators know exactly which NHIS reimbursement categories are underfunded, which drugs are perpetually out of stock, and which patient groups are being turned away. Their knowledge should be feeding policy. Healthcare workers are often the last line of communication between the NHIS and the patient. They are the ones explaining to a mother why her covered medication is not available today. They need to be trained, supported, and genuinely consulted, not just handed new protocols from above. Policy makers must be willing to make difficult decisions about revenue and spending, even when those decisions are not popular.

But perhaps the most important voices (and historically the most excluded) are what we in Ghana sometimes simply call the local man. The farmer in Techiman. The market woman in Kumasi. The young mother in Tamale who walked two hours to reach a clinic. These are not passive recipients of health policy. They are the people who know, in their bones, where the system fails them. Community leaders have social trust that no government agency can manufacture. They can drive enrollment campaigns, dispel misinformation about the NHIS, and hold local facilities accountable in ways that no national directive can. An equitable reform process does not design solutions for communities. It designs them with them

Closing: 
Power, Equity, and Who This Is For

I want to close with something that often gets lost in policy discussions. The NHIS was created to shift the financial burden of illness away from poor individuals and onto a shared, collective system. That is a fundamentally just idea. But systems can drift. Over time, the gaps I've described have meant that the NHIS increasingly serves those who are already better off: people with formal employment, people living near urban facilities, people who can navigate bureaucracy and afford renewals. The poorest are left navigating a patchwork of partial coverage and out-of-pocket costs.

Real reform involves designing reimbursement systems, enrollment processes, and drug supply chains with the most vulnerable users at the center, not as an afterthought. It means giving community leaders, patients, and healthcare workers a genuine voice in how the NHIS evolves And it means being honest that funding universal health coverage requires political courage: the willingness to tax more progressively and to prioritize health as infrastructure, the same way we prioritize roads.
Ghana has built something real with the NHIS. Now the work is to make it real for everyone. 

Thank you for listening.
